# On-page and content SEO

## Start with intent

Determine what the page is supposed to help a person do: learn, compare, buy, navigate, troubleshoot, evaluate, sign up, visit, download, etc. Judge content against that purpose rather than arbitrary word counts.

## Titles

Good titles are unique, concise, descriptive, and aligned with visible page content. Avoid boilerplate overload, keyword repetition, sensationalism, and titles that promise content the page does not deliver.

Do not obsess over a fixed character count; search engines can rewrite/truncate title presentation. Optimize clarity and distinctiveness first.

## Meta descriptions / snippets

A useful meta description summarizes the page accurately and gives a compelling reason to click. It is not a guaranteed displayed snippet and is not a place for keyword stuffing.

## Headings and document structure

Use a clear primary heading and logical hierarchy. Do not force exactly one H1 as a ranking superstition; instead ensure the main topic and structure are understandable to users and assistive technology.

## Main content quality

Look for:

- direct satisfaction of the primary intent;
- original information, experience, examples, tools, data, comparisons, or analysis;
- factual accuracy and source quality;
- enough depth for the task without filler;
- clear update/date handling where freshness matters;
- transparent authorship/review processes where trust matters;
- strong UX around tables, media, examples, navigation, and calls to action;
- consistency between title, heading, body, structured data, and claims.

Do not use a minimum word count as a universal quality gate.

## E-E-A-T

Treat Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trust as a quality-evaluation lens, not a single ranking score.

Trust is especially important. For higher-stakes/YMYL topics, demand stronger sourcing, author/reviewer context, dates, disclaimers where appropriate, and alignment with established evidence.

Do not fabricate credentials or “expert reviewed” labels.

## Information gain

Before recommending a new page, ask what it adds beyond existing results and the site's existing pages. Strong additions may include:

- first-party data;
- original testing;
- expert or practitioner experience;
- useful tools/calculators/templates;
- clearer synthesis of complex material;
- current product/service details;
- visual demonstrations;
- transparent methodology.

A page that merely rewrites existing results is a weak candidate, even if keywords are placed correctly.

## Duplicate and near-duplicate content

Identify whether duplication is:

- legitimate alternate representation needing canonicalization;
- templated boilerplate with sufficient unique main content;
- thin location/programmatic pages lacking distinct value;
- parameter/facet duplication;
- copied/syndicated content;
- accidental environment/version duplication.

Choose the fix based on cause: consolidate, redirect, canonicalize, noindex, improve, or remove.

## Programmatic and AI-generated content

Automation is not inherently disqualifying. The risk is scaled, unoriginal, low-value content created primarily to manipulate search rankings.

Require real user value, data quality, editorial controls, distinct page purpose, sensible indexation, and mechanisms to prevent garbage/duplicate generation.

## Internal content architecture

Recommend new hubs/clusters only when they reflect real user journeys and entity/topic relationships. Avoid creating dozens of overlapping pages for trivial keyword variants.

## Conversion and SXO

A page can rank and still fail users. Inspect whether the result promise, landing-page experience, trust signals, CTA, and next step are coherent. Do not sacrifice clarity or conversion for keyword insertion.
